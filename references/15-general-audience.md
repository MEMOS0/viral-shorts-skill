# 15 — Telling technical stories to people who know nothing

Assume the viewer has no engineering background, is half-listening, and will leave the moment a sentence needs a second hearing.

## 1. What accessible creators do (observed in their published videos) [E — our reading, not measured]

| Creator | Device | Use it as |
|---|---|---|
| Zack D. Films | One object, one consequence, 3D shows the mechanism literally while the narrator speaks in plain present tense; no jargon at all | the visual must make the sentence redundant |
| Mark Rober | Starts from a relatable problem or a person, builds a test you can watch, uses everyday units ("as heavy as a car") | stakes first, physics second |
| Veritasium | Names the viewer's likely wrong intuition, then shows it fail on screen | "you'd think X — but watch" |
| Kurzgesagt / Real Engineering | Scale comparisons and simplified diagrams that drop every detail not needed for the point | one diagram, one idea |
| MrBallen / PlainlyDifficult | A person deciding under time pressure; the technical cause arrives as the twist | human first, mechanism as reveal |

## 2. Rules for the script [R where cited, else E]
1. **One idea per sentence, one new term per video** at most, defined in the same sentence by what it does ("the hanger rod — the steel bar holding the walkway").
2. **Concrete over abstract** (Heath & Heath): "two bars instead of one" beats "a modified connection detail".
3. **Human-scale numbers**: one number per sentence, always with a comparison ("the weight of 30 people on one nut"). Drop numbers that don't change the story.
4. **Intuition first, correction second** (Veritasium pattern): state what a normal person would expect, then break it.
5. **Show, don't define**: if the visual can show it, the narration only points ("watch the nut").
6. **No acronyms** unless spoken daily (NASA ok, PSI no). No passive voice for the cause: say who did what.
7. **Read-aloud test**: a 12-year-old hearing it once can retell the cause in one sentence. If not, rewrite.
8. **Curse-of-knowledge check** [R: Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber 1989]: give the script to a fresh model/person, ask "what caused it?" Any wrong answer = the script, not the listener, failed.

## 3. Choosing tone, rhythm and detail per topic (don't force one formula)

| Story type | Tone | Rhythm | Technical depth | Length that fits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tiny cause, huge effect (hyphen, unit error) | dry irony | fast, punchline end | the one detail only | as short as the story allows (often 30–45 s) |
| Human under pressure (pilot, operator) | tense, present tense | short sentences, silence beat before the decision | minimal; mechanism is the twist | 45–60 s |
| Mechanism reveal (why the bridge swayed) | curious, "watch this" | slower, let the 3D run | one mechanism, shown fully | 45–75 s |
| Cover-up / ignored warning | cold anger | build-up, document reveals | dates + one document | 45–60 s |
| Awe / scale (megastructures) | wonder | wide, held shots | scale comparisons | 30–60 s |
Length follows the story: the v2 data do not show a best length after controlling for channel (`data/analysis_summary.md` §1–4). Cut until every sentence earns its seconds.

## 4. Emotion without manipulation
Respect victims: no gore, no faces of the dead without archive context, no "you won't believe". The engineering lesson is the payoff; the tragedy is not entertainment.
